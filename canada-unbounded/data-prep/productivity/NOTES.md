# Productivity, competition and capital allocation in Canada: evidence notes

Compiled 2026-10-10. This file builds on `../investment/notes.md` and does not repeat it. That file already covers corporate tax cuts vs investment, corporate cash, the OECD Canada/US GDP-per-hour gap, investment per worker, Rogers (2024), Gu (2024), Competition Bureau (2023) headline numbers, the StatCan markups paper (2025) and the competition-intensity paper (2026). Where a figure here overlaps, it is cross-referenced rather than restated.

**Labels.**
- [PRIMARY]: official statistics, central bank, government, regulator, IMF or OECD.
- [PEER-REVIEWED]: journal article. The *International Productivity Monitor* is CSLS's refereed journal.
- [WP]: working or research paper.
- [OWN CALC]: my arithmetic on primary data. The script is named in each case.
- [CONTEXT / THINK TANK] and [MEDIA]: context only.

**Data handling.** Every CSV in this folder is regenerated by a script in `scripts/` (run with `python3 -I` from this folder) from downloads in `raw/`. Raw downloads are untrusted and were not edited.

## Bottom line for the post

1. **Canada's gap with the US is almost entirely a productivity gap, and mostly an efficiency (MFP/TFP) gap rather than a capital-quantity gap.**
   - StatCan/BLS data for 2000–2023: Canadian business-sector labour productivity grew 0.78%/yr against 1.97%/yr in the US. Of the 1.19-point gap, about 1.02 points is weaker MFP and about 0.15 points is weaker capital deepening (§1, A3).
   - StatCan's own Canada–US decomposition (Gu & Willox 2023) gets the same answer. Lower TFP explains 0.87 of the 0.92-point gap in 2001–2019.
   - Since 2019 a capital-deepening gap has opened as well: Canada 0.28 vs US 0.78 points a year.
2. **The living-standards gap now mirrors the productivity gap.** McCormack & Macdonald (StatCan, Mar 2026) find relative productivity down 26% since the late 1990s. In 2025, OECD data put Canada's GDP per capita at 74.3% of the US (current PPP), a gap of about USD 23,100 per person. More than 100% of that gap is output per hour. Canadians actually work slightly more hours per capita.
3. **Dynamism and concentration.**
   - Firm entry is down. The IMF, using StatCan microdata, finds the young-firm share of output/employment fell from 29% to under 20%, and that concentration rose in about half of industries.
   - Key network sectors are highly concentrated (CRTC, IMF and Competition Bureau figures):
     - Big 3 telecoms: 89% of mobile revenue.
     - Six banks: 93–94% of banking assets.
     - Five grocers: about 80% of grocery sales.
     - Two airlines: half to three-quarters of domestic passengers at major airports.
   - StatCan links the post-2001 productivity divergence partly to weak competition in information and cultural industries, including telecom (0.45 points of the 0.92-point gap).
4. **Capital has tilted toward housing.**
   - Residential investment rose from 4.3% of GDP (2000) to 9.4% (2021) and 7.4% (2025). Business M&E plus IPP fell from 8.3% to 5.6%.
   - The US spends 3.8% of GDP on residential and 14.2% on business investment. Canada spends 7.4% and 11.2%.
   - Household real-estate-secured credit takes about 64% of combined household-mortgage and business-loan credit, against 50% in 1990.
   - Land is 37% of national non-financial assets, against 25% in 2000.
5. **Two necessary qualifications for honesty.**
   - Part of the 2022–2025 productivity weakness reflects the population surge. The Bank of Canada (SAP 2026-19) attributes about 70% of the post-pandemic fall below trend to the inflow of non-permanent residents.
   - Interprovincial barriers matter (IMF: up to 7% of GDP), but 78% of businesses that trade interprovincially report no obstacle (StatCan survey, Q1 2026).

### 1c. StatCan's own Canada–US decomposition: Gu & Willox (2023). File: `A5_gu_willox_2023_table1_canada_us_decomposition.csv`

[PEER-REVIEWED / PRIMARY authors] Gu, W. and M. Willox (2023), "The Post-2001 Productivity Growth Divergence between Canada and the United States", *International Productivity Monitor* 45: 28–60. Also published as a StatCan Economic and Social Reports article (Dec 2023).
- https://csls.ca/ipm-archive/ipm-issue-45/the-post-2001-productivity-growth-divergence-between-canada-and-the-united-states/
- PDF: https://csls.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/IPM-Issue-45-2-Gu-Willox.pdf

**Table 1, p.33:**
- The Canada-minus-US labour productivity growth gap widened from −0.46 points (1987–2001) to −0.92 (2001–2019).
- "The relatively lower TFP growth in Canada accounted for 0.87 percentage points of the 0.92 percentage point difference." ICT capital intensity accounted for 0.10.

**Industry contributions (p.34):**
- Information and cultural services and computer/electronics manufacturing "contributed 0.60 percentage points of the 0.92 percentage point difference … (0.45 percentage points from the information and cultural services industry and 0.15 percentage points from computer and electronic products manufacturing)".
- The authors add a caveat: industry contributions are not strictly additive.

**Abstract:**
- "Limits on foreign investment, most notably but not exclusively related to telecommunications, and strong output price growth relative to the United States are key factors."
- Markup estimates suggest "limited competition has significantly reduced the productivity performance of that industry as well as the performances of others that are dependent on its services as intermediate inputs."
- **This is the strongest official-author link between concentration (telecom) and the aggregate productivity gap.**

## 3. Interprovincial trade barriers

### 3a. Estimates of the cost

**IMF 2019** [WP/PRIMARY]. Alvarez, Krznar & Tombe, "Internal Trade in Canada: Case for Liberalization", IMF WP/19/158, 22 Jul 2019.
- https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/WP/Issues/2019/07/22/Internal-Trade-in-Canada-Case-for-Liberalization-47100
- "complete liberalization of internal trade in goods can increase GDP per capita by about 4 percent."
- The PDF itself was blocked (403). The quote is from the IMF landing page summary.

**IMF 2025 Article IV, Box 6** (p.29) and ¶40–41 (p.18) [PRIMARY], same staff report as 1f.
- A gravity model on 230 sectors, 13 provinces and territories, 1997–2021, finds non-geographic barriers of "about 9½ percent tariff equivalents nationally". ¶40 says "about 9 percent", "often exceeding 50 percent in sectors such as health, education, and retail".
- Eliminating them "could raise Canada's real GDP by roughly 7 percent over the long run—about C$210 billion in 2025".
- Services account for "roughly 90 percent" of the gains (staff report). The 27 Jan 2026 IMF article says "roughly four-fifths". **Quote the staff report.**
- Provincial real GDP-per-worker gains: Canada 6.8, Ontario 3.6, Quebec 6.9, BC 5.7, Alberta 4.9, PEI 39.5.
- IMF country-focus article: Díez & Yang, "Canada Can Grow Faster by Unlocking Its Own Market", 27 Jan 2026. https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2026/01/27/cf-canada-can-grow-faster-by-unlocking-its-own-market

**StatCan 2017** [WP/PRIMARY]. Bemrose, Brown & Tweedle, "Going the Distance: Estimating the Effect of Provincial Borders on Trade when Geography Matters", ASB Research Paper No. 394, 14 Sep 2017.
- Daily: https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/170914/dq170914d-eng.htm
- Trade across provinces over 2004–2012 is at the level "that would be expected if a 6.9% tariff were imposed". Within-province regional trade was "53% higher" than cross-border trade.
- **This is the source of the widely quoted "6.9%"**, which is sometimes misattributed to a 2020 StatCan study.

### 3c. 2025 barrier removals

[PRIMARY] Canada.ca, "Advancing internal trade" (modified 2026-01-28). https://www.canada.ca/en/intergovernmental-affairs/services/internal-trade/federal-investments-internal-trade.html
- On 30 Jun 2025 Ottawa "removed all remaining 20 federal exceptions from the CFTA", eliminating "all 53 federal exceptions" since 2017.
- "Since January 1, 2025, federal, provincial and territorial governments have removed 94 or 30%" of "the 296 exceptions to the CFTA" (39 of the 94 were federal).
- The *Free Trade and Labour Mobility in Canada Act* (Part 1 of Bill C-5, *One Canadian Economy Act*; Royal Assent 26 Jun 2025 per secondary summaries, verify on LEGISinfo) came into force 1 Jan 2026. Bill: https://www.parl.ca/DocumentViewer/en/45-1/bill/C-5/first-reading
- The government says barrier removal "could boost GDP by as much as $200 billion … $5,100 per person".
- **No ex-post measurement of 2025 removals exists yet.** The trade-flow tables end in 2022.

### 3d. Business survey. File: `C2_interprovincial_obstacles_survey_q1_2026.csv`

[PRIMARY] StatCan 33-10-1118-01, Canadian Survey on Business Conditions, Q1 2026. The universe is businesses that traded interprovincially in the previous 12 months.
- **78.3% reported no obstacle.**
- Most common "most challenging" obstacle: provincial or territorial tax (6.7%). Next: permits and licences (2.9%) and transportation regulations (2.5%).
- **Good counterweight:** firm-level friction looks modest. The IMF's big numbers come mainly from services, regulated professions and health/education, where many firms never try to sell across provinces.

### 7b. Counterfactuals. File: `G2_productivity_counterfactual_gdp_per_capita.csv`

- **[PRIMARY] Bank of Canada (Vincent, 19 Nov 2025):** "if our productivity growth since 2000 had been similar to that of other G7 countries, our GDP in Canada today would be about 9% higher, which translates to almost $7,000 per person." Footnote 4 says this uses GDP-weighted other-G7 labour productivity growth, 2000–2023.
- **[OWN CALC]** OECD GDP per hour (2020 PPP) grew 21.1% in Canada from 2000 to 2025, against 50.6% in the US and 42.0% in the OECD.
  - Had Canada matched the US, GDP per hour would be 24.3% higher: about **$19,000 per person** at 2025 GDP per capita ($78,008).
  - Matching the OECD aggregate gives +17.2%, or about $13,400.
  - This is mechanical. It holds hours fixed and ignores general-equilibrium effects. **Prefer the Bank of Canada number in the text.**
- **Wages:** the link between productivity and average pay is covered in `../labour/notes.md` §5. Since about 1997, average real compensation per hour has roughly tracked business-sector productivity, and the median has lagged. So productivity gains do reach average pay, but not evenly.
