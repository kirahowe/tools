## 1. Colonial roots of concentrated landholding

### 1a. Seigneurial system (New France / Lower Canada)
- The system was legally abolished in **1854**. Seigneurial cadastres were then drawn up to fix the indemnities paid to seigneurs [peer-reviewed review: F. Roy, review of Grenier & Morissette (eds.), *Nouveaux regards en histoire seigneuriale au Québec* (Septentrion 2016), *Cahiers de géographie du Québec* 61(173), 2017, pp. 398–399, https://www.erudit.org/fr/revues/cgq/2017-v61-n173-cgq03845/1049393ar/].
- Rents (rentes constituées) survived well past 1854:
  - The **Syndicat national du rachat des rentes seigneuriales was created in 1935**.
  - It paid off the remaining rentes. The sums were converted into a **municipal property tax paid 1941–1981** (same review).
  - The Syndicat had recovered its money "dès le début des années 1970".
  - A secondary search summary of another Érudit article (M. Morissette) says about **65,000 families** still owed rent in 1940. **Not verified.**
- Not retrieved: the founding date (Company of One Hundred Associates, 1627) and the number of seigneuries. The Canadian Encyclopedia was blocked.

### 1b. Hudson's Bay Company: 1670 charter and 1869–70 surrender
- **Charter:** Charles II, 1670. Exclusive trade over lands draining into Hudson Bay, about 3.9 million km² ("five times larger than France").
  - Canada acquired Rupert's Land for **£300,000 (CAD$1.5M)**, which the source calls "the largest real estate transaction (by land area) in the country's history" [ref: Canadian Encyclopedia, "Rupert's Land", McIntosh & Smith, last edited 2025-02-21, via WebFetch summary].
- **The order is primary:** *Order of Her Majesty in Council admitting Rupert's Land and the North-Western Territory into the union*, dated **23 June 1870**, effective **15 July 1870** [primary: Justice Canada, Constitution Acts, Enactment No. 3, https://www.justice.gc.ca/eng/rp-pr/csj-sjc/constitution/lawreg-loireg/p1t31.html].
  - "Canada is to pay to the Company 300,000l. when Rupert's Land is transferred". Payment went into the Bank of England within six months, at 5% interest from acceptance.
  - The Company kept blocks around its posts: 500 acres each at Upper and Lower Fort Garry in one schedule. In the Red River Territory the blocks were "not to exceed 50,000 acres" in aggregate.
  - **One-twentieth of the Fertile Belt is verified:** "The Company may, for fifty years after the surrender, claim in any township or district within the Fertile Belt … grants of land not exceeding one twentieth part of the land so set out."
  - The Fertile Belt is bounded by the US border, the Rocky Mountains, the North Saskatchewan River, and Lake Winnipeg / Lake of the Woods.
  - The draft surrender terms were approved by the Governor General on **5 July 1869**.
- **The end of the firm** [secondary: CBC, BNN Bloomberg]:
  - HBC filed under the CCAA on **7 March 2025** and closed all remaining stores on **1 June 2025**.
  - Canadian Tire bought the brand and IP, including the stripes, for about **$30M**. The deal was announced 15 May 2025 and approved by the court 3 June 2025.
  - Sources: https://www.cbc.ca/1.7551035 ; https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/business/2025/12/11/a-timeline-of-how-hudsons-bay-has-been-winding-down/
  - This is a useful counterpoint: the oldest chartered fortune did not endure as a family dynasty.

### 1c. Clergy and Crown Reserves; Family Compact; Durham Report (1839)
Main primary source: **Lord Durham's Report** (Report on the Affairs of British North America, dated 31 January 1839, submitted February 1839). I used the C.P. Lucas edition (Oxford 1912), vol. 2, full text [primary: https://www.fadedpage.com/showbook.php?pid=20210933; text file https://www.fadedpage.com/books/20210933/20210933.txt].

- **Clergy reserves.** Durham, Lucas ed. pp. 220–221:
  - "The system of clergy reserves was established by the act of 1791, commonly called the Constitutional Act, which directed that, in respect of all grants made by the Crown, a quantity equal to **one-seventh** of the land so granted should be reserved for the clergy."
  - He adds that one-seventh of grants equals one-eighth of each township. This resolves the "one-seventh vs one-eighth" discrepancy that Britannica shows.
  - In Upper Canada the practice instead reserved "a seventh of all the land … equal to a sixth of the land granted", an excess of 300,000 acres.
  - Lower Canada: 673,567 acres went to the clergy instead of 446,000.
  - Clergy reserves sales in Upper Canada totalled £317,000.
  - "Upwards of 3,000,000 acres" of reserves lay in lots of 200 acres "scattered at regular intervals … remaining, with few exceptions, entirely wild to this day."
- **Crown reserves.** Durham (Appendix) on a township of 80,000 acres: "one-seventh is reserved for the clergy and one-seventh for the Crown". This is how instructions were practised, not wording from the 1791 Act. I did not retrieve the Act's own text on Crown reserves.
- **Secularization:** an Imperial Act of 1853 enabled the Canadian Parliament to legislate, and **the Clergy Reserves were secularized in 1854** (Lucas editorial note 29).
- **Family Compact.** Durham, p. 148, describes a party that "possessed almost all the highest public offices". Key passage:
  - "The bench, the magistracy, the high offices of the Episcopal Church, and a great part of the legal profession, are filled by the adherents of this party: **by grant or purchase, they have acquired nearly the whole of the waste lands of the Province**; they are all-powerful in the chartered banks, and, till lately, shared among themselves almost exclusively all offices of trust and profit."
  - Durham also noted "there is, in truth, very little of family connexion among the persons thus united." Good for balance: the "Compact" was an oligarchy, not literally a family.
- Canadian Encyclopedia (David Mills, via WebFetch summary):
  - Leading figures were John Beverley Robinson and John Strachan.
  - The power base was the Councils (which controlled land grants "along partisan lines"), the Church of England, the Canada Company, the Law Society and the **Bank of Upper Canada**.
  - The term was first used by Marshall Spring Bidwell (8 Sept 1828) and popularized by W.L. Mackenzie in 1833.
- **Who got the land in Upper Canada** (Durham pp. 222–223; `durham_upper_canada_land_grants_1839.csv`):

  | Recipient | Acres |
  |---|---|
  | U.E. Loyalists and children | 3,200,000 |
  | Militiamen | 730,000 |
  | Discharged soldiers and sailors | 450,000 |
  | Magistrates and barristers | 255,000 |
  | **Executive councillors and their families** | **136,000** |
  | **Five legislative councillors and their families** | **50,000** |
  | Survey contractors | 264,000 |
  | Colonel Talbot | 48,520 |
  | Heirs of General Brock | 12,000 |

  - "Making altogether, with the clergy reserves, nearly half of all the surveyed land in the Province."
  - "Perhaps less than a tenth" of granted land had been occupied by settlers.
  - In Lower Canada: 1,457,209 acres went to "leaders of townships" and about 48,000 acres to Governor Milnes. **Prince Edward Island**, about 1,400,000 acres, "was alienated in one day".
  - Of about 17,000,000 surveyed acres in Upper Canada, less than 1,600,000 remained unappropriated: "the whole of the public lands in Upper Canada have been alienated by the Government."
- **Château Clique:** I found no primary or academic text. Durham describes the Lower Canada equivalent as "a powerful party, linked together not only by common party interests, but by personal ties." He did not use the term "Château Clique" in the passages I read. **Gap.**

### 1d. Canadian Pacific Railway land grant (1881)
- "A contract, dated the 21st October, 1880" was "by an Act passed in the Session of 1881, 44 Victoria, chapter 1, approved of and ratified by Parliament." The company was incorporated by letters patent on **16 February 1881**.
- "A subsidy was, by the Act of 1881, granted to the company, consisting of **$25,000,000 in money, and 25,000,000 acres of land**." [primary: *Statistical Year-Book of Canada 1886*, p. 301, Statistics Canada historical collection, https://www66.statcan.gc.ca/eng/1886/188603150301_p.%20301.pdf]
- A Wikisource transcription of the Act's Schedule exists: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/An_Act_respecting_the_Canadian_Pacific_Railway/Schedule. It is not read in full here.
- The land was to come from odd-numbered sections in a belt along the line, with a right to reject land "not fairly fit for settlement". That comes from a secondary search summary of an 1881 newspaper notice; verify it against the Act before quoting.
- **Not verified:** how the land grant fed later CP fortunes (e.g. mineral rights → PanCanadian Petroleum). The Alberta Source archive page (https://wayback.archive-it.org/2217/20101208163031/http:/www.albertasource.ca/petroleum/featured_article/cpr_land_grants.html) is a lead.

### 1e. Dominion Lands Act (1872)
- The 1872 (original) Act offered "a free homestead of **160 acres** (also known as a quarter-section) for a **$10 registration fee**." "The settler must cultivate and reside on the land for three years." The minimum age was 21 before 1874 [primary/archival: Saskatchewan Archives, https://www.saskarchives.com/node/82].
- Contrast with 1c and 1d: homesteaders got 160 acres, while the CPR got 25,000,000 acres and the HBC up to 1/20 of the fertile belt.

### 1f. Indigenous dispossession: numbered treaties
- "The Crown negotiated **eleven treaties between 1871 and 1921**." The page calls them "land surrenders on a huge scale" from Lake of the Woods to the Rockies to the Beaufort Sea, given "in exchange for Aboriginal title" [primary: Crown-Indigenous Relations and Northern Affairs Canada, https://www.rcaanc-cirnac.gc.ca/eng/1360948213124/1544620003549].
- Reserve land formulas:
  - Treaties 1, 2 and 5: **160 acres per family of five**.
  - Treaties 3 and 6: **640 acres per family of five**.
- Annuities: $3 under Treaties 1–2, raised to $5 in 1875; $5 under the others; $4 under Treaty 9.
- **[calc] Framing comparison:** a family of five under Treaties 1, 2 and 5 received 160 acres, the same as one settler homestead under the Dominion Lands Act. The CPR received 25,000,000 acres. Present this as context only; treaty interpretation is contested.
- The CIRNAC page notes that although "the Dominion had purchased the title for Rupert's Land, it had no way of exerting its influence or jurisdiction over the area."

### 1g. Fortunes traceable to colonial privilege (verifiable only)
- **John Redpath** [ref: Dictionary of Canadian Biography (DCB), G. Tulchinsky, vol. 9, https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/redpath_john_9E.html]:
  - He made his fortune first as a public-works contractor on the Lachine and Rideau canals.
  - He bought canal-side land "from the Sulpicians" (the seigneurs of Montreal).
  - In **1854** he began Canada's first sugar refinery and put £40,000 into it.
  - It opened in 1855 "under the protection of favourable tariffs conveniently established by the Canadian government in 1855 – the year Redpath's factory opened."
  - This is a documented case of state contracts plus tariff protection plus seigneurial land.
- **John Molson** [ref: DCB, Alfred Dubuc, vol. 7, https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/molson_john_1763_1836_7E.html]:
  - He bought the brewery at a creditor's auction (5 Jan 1785) and took over management on **31 May 1786**.
  - The Molson fortune is merchant/industrial, not a land grant. Don't overstate a "Crown land" link.
- **HBC and CPR:** see 1b and 1d. The HBC company did not become a family dynasty.
- **Gap:** no verified primary link found between Family Compact land grants and any fortune that still exists. Treat the land-grant → modern-dynasty thread as **thematic, not genealogical**, except where a source says otherwise.

## 3. Dual-class shares in Canada

### 3a. Counts (`dual_class_canada_counts.csv`)
- IGOPP's chair, Yvan Allaire, wrote **[advocacy: IGOPP supports dual class with safeguards]**:
  - "Some **69** dual-class companies are now listed on the Toronto Stock Exchange, down from **100 in 2005**."
  - "Since 2005, only 23 Canadian companies went public with dual-class shares," and 16 of those 23 later converted.
  - Source: Financial Post op-ed reposted 2019-03-06, https://igopp.org/en/?p=10939.
  - The same piece claims the 54 surviving dual-class firms returned **8.98%/yr** over 10 years vs **5.06%** for the S&P/TSX and 6.0% for the TSX 60. That is IGOPP's own unaudited calculation. **Label it as advocacy; it has survivorship bias** (54 of 69 survived).
- Allaire's estimate: about **13% of the 253** S&P/TSX Composite companies in 2008 had some dual-class form [secondary: Globe and Mail 2010, https://www.theglobeandmail.com/report-on-business/do-your-shares-have-the-power/article1215251].
- **Gap:** no current (2024–26) authoritative count was found. S&P/TSX constituent share-class data or a recent Clarkson Centre/CCGG study would be needed.
- Canadian Coalition for Good Governance (CCGG), 2013 dual-class policy: chose disclosure-plus-principles over prohibition [secondary summary]. Not retrieved.

### 3b. Evidence on performance and valuation
- **Canada, counter-evidence:** Jog, Zhu & Dutta (2010), *Corporate Governance: An International Review* 18(5):415–437. Their 10-year TSX panel (1996–2005) finds **no evidence** that restricted-voting firms have lower firm value, operating performance or stock performance, and no expropriation via M&A or dividends. They credit Canada's minority-protection environment and say "no generalization is warranted" [peer-reviewed; abstract via vLex, https://international.vlex.com/vid/impact-of-restricted-voting-855643951].
- Amoako-Adu & Smith (2001), "Dual class firms: Capitalization, ownership structure and recapitalization back into single class," *Journal of Banking & Finance* 25(6):1083–1111. Per a partial abstract, "the combination of a large controlling shareholder with family interests," rather than concentrated ownership as such, leads to dual-class capitalization. The sample country was not confirmed. **Verify before use.**
- **US:**
  - Gompers, Ishii & Metrick (2010), *RFS* 23(3):1051–1088: firm value rises with insiders' cash-flow rights and falls with their voting rights (OLS; IV results weaker) [https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v23y2010i3p1051-1088.html].
  - Smart, Thirumalai & Zutter (2008), *JAE* 45(1):94–115: dual-class firms trade at lower prices at IPO and for at least 5 years, "do not foreshadow abnormally low stock or accounting returns"; CEO turnover is insensitive to performance; unifications produce "statistically and economically significant value gains" [https://eprints.exchange.isb.edu/id/eprint/118/].
  - Cremers, Lauterbach & Pajuste (ECGI WP, Nov 2018): dual-class firms have **higher** valuations around IPO; the premium "tends to dissipate" while the voting-equity wedge grows; "mildly support[s]" age-based sunsets [https://ecgi.global/sites/default/files/The%20Life-Cycle%20of%20Dual%20Class%20Firm%20Valuations-%20Paper.pdf].
  - Bebchuk & Kastiel (2017), *Virginia Law Review* 103:585–631: argue against perpetual dual class and for sunsets of about 10–15 years [https://virginialawreview.org/articles/untenable-case-perpetual-dual-class-stock/].
- **Link to dynasties:** Villalonga & Amit (2006) find that **37% of first-generation family firms but 56% of second-and-later-generation firms use control-enhancing mechanisms**, which "reduce the founder's premium."
- Nenova (2003), as reported in MWY 2005: private benefits of control in Canada are **under 4% of firm value**, low by world standards. This supports the "Canada protects minorities" view.

## 4. Do heir-controlled firms underperform? (`family_control_performance_studies.csv`)

### 4a. Morck, Stangeland & Yeung (2000), "The Canadian Disease?"
Source: NBER volume *Concentrated Corporate Ownership*, ch. 11, pp. 319–372; WP 6814, Nov 1998 [peer-reviewed chapter, read in full: https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c9014/c9014.pdf].

- **Cross-country** (41 countries, 1993 Forbes; US and UK excluded from regressions):
  - Growth is positively related to self-made billionaire wealth/GDP and negatively related to heir billionaire wealth/GDP, controlling for initial income, capital accumulation and education.
  - The coefficient on heir wealth/GDP is −0.292. Moving from zero to the 75th percentile (3.293% of GDP) means "a slowdown of 0.962 percent per year"; the 90th percentile (6.667%) means "1.95 percent per year". Mean growth in the sample is 4.16%/yr.
- **Canadian firm level:**
  - Sample: 246 public firms in the 1988 Financial Post 500. Of these, **44 heir-controlled (20% of sales)**, 27 entrepreneur-controlled, and **only 53 widely held** at a 20% voting threshold. The average largest stake is about 50%.
  - Using 1984–89 data, benchmarked to US 3-digit industry averages and to size/age-matched US firms: "Control by heirs is associated with lower returns on sales and assets and with growth that is less than or equal to that observed in other comparable firms."
  - "Founder control is associated with earnings lower than those in widely held firms but higher than those in heir-controlled firms and with growth greater than or equal to that of widely held firms."
  - Heir-controlled firms have **significantly lower labour intensity** (more capital per worker), consistent with cheaper capital through pyramids. They also **invest less in R&D**.
  - Cross-country, enterprise R&D is negatively related to heir wealth.
- **FTA natural experiment:** cumulative abnormal returns from 10 Nov to 21 Nov 1988, the surprise Conservative majority that ensured FTA ratification.
  - "Heir-controlled companies appear most adversely affected, while firms controlled by business entrepreneurs appear to gain the most."
  - After the FTA, heir firms' capital-to-labour ratios converged with other firms', and departing entrepreneurs' firms tended to become widely held rather than heir-controlled.
- **Caveats in the paper itself:**
  - Pyramid membership did not worsen the result: "It does not, however, substantiate our concern that control pyramids might worsen the damage."
  - Footnote 10: Bronfman pyramid firms "performed no worse than other similar firms but appear to follow higher-risk strategies."
  - The authors say causality is left open.
  - Unexplained: entrepreneur wealth is also negatively correlated with enterprise R&D.
- **Definition of the "Canadian disease":** "poor overall management quality, capital markets and institutions that channel money to large, established family firms, a dearth of innovation that locks in the status quo, and political rent seeking that deters entry."

### 4b. Morck, Wolfenzon & Yeung (2005), *JEL* 43(3)
"Corporate Governance, Economic Entrenchment, and Growth" [peer-reviewed; NBER WP 10692 text: https://www.nber.org/papers/w10692].
- Pyramids, cross-holdings and super-voting shares let families control "without making a commensurate capital investment."
- "Political influence is plausibly related to what one controls, rather than what one owns." They call this **economic entrenchment**.
- Canada in their Table 2 (La Porta et al. 1999): among the 20 largest firms, 60% are widely held and 25% family-controlled at a 20% threshold. Attig, Gadhoum & Lang (2003) find much more family control (26–47%) using broader samples.
- They report Smith & Amoako-Adu (1999) as finding Canadian "share price drops when heirs take charge — especially if the heirs are young."
- **Desmarais ties:** "Paul Desmarais, the controlling shareholder of one of Canada's largest pyramidal groups, is also a closely related in-law of former Prime Minister Jean Chrétien and the longtime business associate of the current Prime Minister, Paul Martin." They add, of such ties generally, "there is no hint of corruption in many of these family ties."
- Faccio (2003): firms that are 7.76% of world market capitalization are run by relatives of political leaders.

### 4c. Smith & Amoako-Adu (1999)
*Journal of Corporate Finance* 5(4):341–368, DOI 10.1016/S0929-1199(99)00010-3 (DOI via Crossref).
- Canadian family-controlled firms; event study of successions.
- **The abstract could not be retrieved** (ScienceDirect 403). The finding above is as summarized by MWY 2005.
- Do not quote sample sizes or magnitudes until the paper is read.

### 4d. International causal and descriptive evidence
- **Pérez-González (2006)**, *AER* 96(5):1559–1588. US CEO successions: firms whose incoming CEO is related to the departing CEO, founder or large shareholder underperform in operating ROA and market-to-book. The gap is larger when the family CEO did not attend a selective college. Interpreted as "wasteful nepotism" [AEA abstract page].
- **Bennedsen, Nielsen, Pérez-González & Wolfenzon (2007)**, *QJE* 122(2) (NBER WP 12356). Denmark; instrument = gender of the departing CEO's first-born child.
  - "Family successions have a large negative causal impact on firm performance: operating profitability on assets falls by **at least four percentage points** around CEO transitions."
  - The effect is larger in fast-growing, skill-intensive industries and larger firms. IV estimates exceed OLS.
- **Villalonga & Amit (2006)**, *JFE* 80(2):385–417. Fortune 500, 1994–2000.
  - "Family ownership creates value only when the founder serves as CEO … or as Chairman with a hired CEO."
  - "When descendants serve as CEOs, firm value is destroyed."
- **Counter-evidence: Anderson & Reeb (2003)**, *JF* 58(3):1301–1328. S&P 500, 1992–99, 403 firms, 141 family firms (35%).
  - "Contrary to our conjecture, we find family firms perform better than nonfamily firms"; family-CEO firms do better than outside-CEO firms [abstract via secondary summaries].
  - Reconciliation: Villalonga & Amit show the premium is concentrated in **founder-led** firms. Anderson & Reeb's 2004 *ASQ* paper finds family firms with few independent directors perform worse.
- **Balance line for the essay:** the evidence separates **founders** (often positive) from **heirs** (generally negative, causally so in Denmark). Canada-specific evidence on heirs (MSY; Smith & Amoako-Adu) is negative. Canada-specific evidence on **dual-class per se** (Jog et al.) is neutral.

### 4e. Does inherited wealth sit in less productive assets? (handle carefully)
- **No direct study was found** showing that inherited wealth is systematically held in "less productive" assets. The evidence is indirect and partly cuts the other way.
- **MSY (Canada):** heir-controlled firms are more capital-intensive and do less R&D, a firm-level "old capital" bias. They argue entrenched billionaires "have a vested interest in preserving the value of old capital."
- **Fagereng, Guiso, Malacrino & Pistaferri** (NBER WP 22822; *Econometrica* 2020), Norway:
  - Returns to wealth differ by about 500 bp between the 10th and 90th percentiles. Returns are positively correlated with wealth, have a persistent individual component, and the permanent component is "(mildly) correlated across generations."
  - This shows persistence. It does **not** show low returns for heirs [https://www.nber.org/papers/w22822].
- **Nekoei & Seim (2023)**, *REStud* 90(1):463–498, Sweden:
  - "The average heir depletes her inheritance within a decade while the inheritances of wealthy heirs remain intact," because of "different rates of return on inherited wealth," not consumption or labour responses.
  - Inheritances first reduce relative inequality, then **increase** it within a decade.
  - Inheritance taxation reduces long-run inequality "only through the taxation of wealthy heirs."
  - **This contradicts the "heirs earn low returns" story at the top.**
- **Elinder, Erixson & Waldenström (2018)**, *J. Public Econ.*, Sweden registers: inheritances **reduce** relative wealth inequality (Gini down 5–10% in the WP version) but increase absolute dispersion. The mobility effect is short-lived.
- **Holtz-Eakin, Joulfaian & Rosen (1993)**, *QJE* 108(2) ("Carnegie Conjecture"; NBER WP 4118): a single person inheriting over $150,000 is "roughly four times more likely to leave the labor force" than one inheriting under $25,000. This is evidence on labour supply, not on asset productivity.
- r > g (Piketty) is context only. Not researched here.

## 5. Inheritance in Canada

- **StatCan, "Familial support in entering the Canadian housing market"**, *Economic and Social Reports* 36-28-0001, released **2025-03-26**, based on the 2023 SFS [primary: https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/36-28-0001/2025003/article/00001-eng.htm; DOI 10.25318/36280001202500300001-eng].
  - "In 2019, 3 in 10 homeowners reported receiving an inheritance at a median value of $67,000, while 2 in 10 renters received a median value of $33,000. … By 2023, the median inheritance Canadian homeowners received had risen to **$85,100**."
  - 5% of families live in a home acquired in whole or part by gift or inheritance. 9% had some of their down payment from a gift or inheritance. With borrowing from family included, **4 in 10 homeowners** benefited from familial support.
  - **Correction to secondary press:** the "18%" figure is for **young families (primary earner under 35)** having received a general inheritance, **not** "18% of all Canadians" as wealthprofessional.ca reported.
  - Under-35 homeowners: 18% got part of their down payment as a gift, and 6% live in a gifted home. The median young homeowner's inheritance was about $45,000 more than young renters'.
- **Peer-reviewed** (prior notes §6h): a 2024 SFS 2005–2019 study, *Enfances Familles Générations* no. 46, finds inheritance more likely and larger for higher-status households, reproducing inequality; it rises partly because of ageing [https://releve.erudit.org/fr/revues/efg/2024-n46-efg09848/1116166ar/resume/].
- **No Canadian estimate of the share of wealth that is inherited** was found. **Gap.** Candidates to check: Davies & Di Matteo; StatCan SFS microdata (inheritance questions); a PBO request.
- **Coming transfer:** CIBC (2016) and CPA Canada (2023) estimates are in prior notes §6h. Not repeated.
- **No inheritance tax** [primary: OECD, *Inheritance Taxation in OECD Countries*, presentation May 2021, https://www.oecd.org/tax/tax-policy/inheritance-taxation-in-oecd-countries-presentation-may-2021.pdf; report: https://doi.org/10.1787/e2879a7d-en]:
  - "**24 of 37 OECD countries** levy an inheritance or an estate tax."
  - Canada is listed under "Repealed inheritance or estate taxes" with **Canada (1972)**, alongside Australia (1979), Israel (1980), Mexico (1961), New Zealand (1992), Austria (2008), Czech Republic (2014), Norway (2014), Slovak Republic (2004) and Sweden (2004).
  - Where these taxes exist they raise "only 0.5% of total tax revenues" on average.
  - The same deck charts inheritances by wealth quintile for Canada, among others. The values were not extracted.
  - Quebec succession duties ended in 1985 and deemed disposition applies at death: see context notes §4.

## 6. Political dynasties

### 6a. Canadian quantitative evidence
Source: **Jacques P. Gagnon, "Elementary Kinship Structures in Parliament since 1867"**, *Canadian Parliamentary Review* 34(3), Autumn 2011, built from Library of Parliament PARLINFO kinship data [https://www.revparlcan.ca/?p=3907; `parliament_kinship_counts_gagnon2011.csv`].

- **Brothers:** 105 parliamentarians (92 MPs, 13 senators) from 50 families have sat with brothers.
  - New brothers by period: 48 (1867–96), 33 (1896–1935), 15 (1935–68), 9 (1968–2008).
- **Fathers and sons:** 133 sons from 127 families, 260 parliamentarians (210 MPs, 50 senators). The corrected unique count is 256, since four are both son and father.
  - Entries by period: **94 (1867–96), 68 (1896–1935), 57 (1935–68), 37 (1968–2011)**.
  - "**Over 100**" father–son pairs involved a son **inheriting the riding**. Quebec has the most, then Ontario and the Maritimes.
  - 85 sons began their careers younger than their fathers did.
- About 20 married couples have sat; in half, the riding passed from husband to wife. 8 daughters.
- MPs sitting in 2011 whose fathers were MPs: **Maxime Bernier, Jack Layton, Dominic LeBlanc, Peter MacKay, Geoff Regan, Mark Strahl, Justin Trudeau**. Marion Dewar's son Paul Dewar also sat.
- **The trend is clearly downward.** Gagnon: "the trend is toward fewer fathers and sons over time."
- **Caveats:**
  - No denominator: dynastic **share** of all parliamentarians per period was not computed. ParlInfo was Cloudflare-blocked, so I could not extract the full "family relationships" list or total MPs per period.

### 6c. Comparative research
- **Dal Bó, Dal Bó & Snyder (2009)**, "Political Dynasties," *REStud* 76(1) (NBER WP 13122) [https://www.nber.org/papers/w13122]. US Congress since 1789:
  - "approximately **8.7%** of legislators had previous relatives in office", and 8.5% had relatives entering later.
  - Using RD and IV designs, holding power for more than one term raises the chance a relative later enters Congress by **about 70%** (3.1–5.2 percentage points).
  - "In politics, power begets power."
  - The paper also covers historical trends and dynastic bias relative to other occupations. Those results were not extracted here.
- **Daniel M. Smith**, *Dynasties and Democracy: The Inherited Incumbency Advantage in Japan* (Stanford UP, 2018). Per the publisher description: legislator-level data from **12 democracies**; dynasts are over a quarter of Japan's legislators recently, but in most democracies they "rarely exceed ten percent" and decline over time.
  - Whether Canada is among the 12, and its figure, was **not retrieved** (sup.org 403).
- **Querubín (2016)**, "Family and Politics: Dynastic Persistence in the Philippines," *Quarterly Journal of Political Science* 11(2), DOI 10.1561/100.00014182. Abstract not retrieved.
